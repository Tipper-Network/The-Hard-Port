# Entity Assessment and Engagement Boundaries

> **Status:** `candidate`, `non_canonical`  
> **Philosophical source:** [`THP-PH-003`](../../00-foundations/THP-VALUES-PRINCIPLES-WILL.md) §14–§16

## Legitimate Authority

THP's authority within an engagement depends on:

- explicit invitation;
- informed consent;
- a defined scope;
- relevant competence;
- transparency regarding the process;
- and the continuing right of the participant to reject, limit, or withdraw.

Access to private information does not independently create authority.

The ability to produce a desired result does not independently create authority.

## Refusal of Engagement

THP may refuse to work with a person or entity.

**Refusal is not a judgment that the client's entire value system is wrong.**

Refusal means THP will not participate in this engagement under current conditions — not that the participant lacks legitimate values, identity, or worth.

### Refusal conditions

THP may refuse when:

- the participant is unwilling to examine assumptions relevant to scope;
- the participant refuses to participate honestly in the process;
- the participant insists on a predetermined answer;
- the participant demands symptom relief without examination of underlying patterns;
- expectations exceed THP's competence or defined scope;
- informed consent cannot be established;
- foreseeable harm to affected parties or THP personnel is likely;
- or the engagement is fundamentally incompatible with THP's own values and responsibilities.

### What refusal must not claim

- That the participant's entire value structure is invalid
- That THP possesses authority over the participant's personal values without consent
- That private information accessed during discovery grants ongoing authority beyond scope

### What refusal may record

- Specific incompatibility with THP participation boundaries
- Missing consent, scope, or honesty conditions
- Foreseeable harm or competence limits
- Which THP values or responsibilities would be compromised by proceeding

## Rights, Safety, and Sovereignty Review

When a value appears to conflict with another person's rights, safety, or sovereignty, investigate before concluding:

- how the value is defined;
- which principle translated it into action;
- who possessed decision authority;
- what alternatives were considered;
- what harms were foreseeable;
- whether affected parties could refuse.

Do not declare an entire value structure wrong without completing this distinction (THP-PH-003 §15).
