# Instinct — The Godin Checks

Read `.cursor/rules/00-brand-core.mdc` first.

Seth Godin's core claim: in a crowded market, "safe" is the riskiest thing you can be. A boring,
average, "fine" business is invisible; a remarkable one gets talked about. Run these checks on
anything new.

## The Purple Cow test

Would a visitor screenshot this and send it to a friend, unprompted? If the honest answer is
"no, but it's solid and clear," it's not done yet — solid and clear is the bar this brand exists
to clear by a mile, not meet exactly.

## The smallest viable audience instinct

Godin's move is never "appeal to everyone" — it's find the smallest group who would care the
most, and speak only to them. Hard Port already does this instinctively (gatekeeping the free
slots, "not picked? you can always just pay us") — protect that instinct anywhere new copy gets
written. If a line is written to avoid alienating anyone, it will land with no one. Widening the
audience is a downgrade, not growth.

## The "people like us do things like this" instinct

The strongest conversion lever isn't convincing someone Hard Port is good — it's making the
reader feel like applying is simply what a business owner "like this" does. Copy should
reinforce identity ("if you're the kind of owner who's done being fine...") more than it lists
features. Anywhere the copy is listing what the agency does instead of who the reader is
becoming, that's a miss.

## The tension instinct

Good marketing creates a tension that only resolves through action — Godin's version of "the
gap." The site already has this (the fog between "here" and "there") — any new section should
be checked for whether it adds to that tension or accidentally resolves it too early. A
reassuring section mid-funnel kills the tension the whole page is built on; hold it until the
CTA.
